Sky Timelapses

A record of what happened in the sky over the property, told a few different ways.

The Ground Truth Network's cameras watch the whole sky from sunrise to sunset, every day. Each video is the story of one stretch of time: what the clouds did, where weather came from, and what the hilltop weather station recorded while it happened. Different skies tell their story best in different views:

  • Look around (360°): drag or swipe to face any direction while the day plays. Best for storms coming over the hills, fog in the valleys, and sunrise and sunset along the horizon. The compass shows which way you're facing; the readout shows the temperature and wind at that moment.
  • Look up (overhead): the whole dome of the sky at once. Best for cloud cover building and clearing, and clouds passing straight over.
  • In the rain: when rain is falling on the camera, the centre of the overhead view shows the station's rain and wind instead. The camera doesn't stop observing when it rains; it changes how it observes.

The fisheye camera looks straight up and sees the entire dome of the sky at once. Each video is one full day, from first light to dark.

Same sky, different questions

One day of sky and weather readings answers different questions depending on who is asking.

Pilots

Which way the clouds are moving, how low they are sitting over the hills, and whether the wind at the field matches what the sky is doing. A look before launch and a record after the flight.

Storm watchers

Where a storm built, which direction it came from, how fast it crossed the hills, and how much rain the station measured when it arrived.

Visitors and photographers

When the sunrise, sunset, and fog were worth getting up for, and which way to face to see them.

Property owners and insurers

A timestamped record of what hit the property and when: the storm, the wind, the rain total. Useful after hail, wind damage, or flooding.

Ranchers and land managers

How much rain actually fell here versus the forecast, how long the rain lasted, and when the fog lifted.

Researchers

Whole-sky footage from the same site every day, lined up with the weather station readings taken at the same moments.
